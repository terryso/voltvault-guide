Why Hotel USB Ports Won&#x27;t Charge Your Laptop

The USB port on a hotel nightstand looks convenient. So does the one on the TV, and the one in the alarm clock. Plug in after a late check-in and you expect a full laptop and a full phone by breakfast. Most mornings the phone has only crept forward, and the laptop has not charged.

Those ports are low-power USB outputs, not small wall chargers. Most are USB-A at about 5W. A newer room may add USB-C at 15W or less, far below the USB Power Delivery a laptop expects. The practical fix is one real AC outlet plus a compact 65W USB-C PD GaN charger that can charge a laptop and a phone from that single socket. Many rooms hide a usable outlet behind the bed, the desk, or the TV stand.

What the furniture port actually supplies

A lamp, a clock, and a TV are not power supplies with a wattage chart. The USB jack shares the circuit that already runs the device.

  • USB-A at about 5W. Five volts and roughly 1 amp, molded into a nightstand lamp or an alarm clock.
  • USB-A marked 2.1A. About 10W on the sticker. Heat and a supply shared with the lamp often deliver less.
  • A weak USB-C port. Newer TVs and desk panels sometimes offer 5–15W. The shape does not mean Power Delivery. Without 15V or 20V, a laptop will not treat it as a charger.

    • A real USB-A wall charger still lives in the low teens of watts. Furniture ports sit under that. The connector gap, when both ports are on an actual charger, is in our USB-C vs USB-A guide. In a hotel, a lamp jack is a phone trickle, not a computer supply.

    Why a laptop will not charge from it

    Laptop USB-C charging is a handshake. Power Delivery picks a voltage — often 15V or 20V — commonly 45–65W for an ultrabook and 65–100W for a larger laptop. A 5W port cannot offer those voltages.

    1. 1. Nothing. No valid source, so the percentage stays put.
  • 2. A losing trickle. A few watts in, more drawn by the screen and CPU, so the battery still falls.
  • 3. A slow climb only while asleep, and only if the port has the power. Nightstand ports almost never do.

    • That is a different limit from charging a laptop with a power bank, where the bank's PD output or its watt-hours run out. Here the port was never a laptop source.

    What the same port does to a phone overnight

    Five watts look like progress on a phone and fail a laptop. A phone battery is often 12–18Wh. At a true 5W, empty to full takes on the order of three to four hours before heat, conversion loss, and notifications that wake the screen. Furniture ports often deliver less than the sticker.

    • From about 40% you may wake near 70%: just ahead of idle drain.
  • From about 15% after a travel day you may wake near half. Enough for a map, tight if the phone is also a hotspot.
  • A 25–45W fast charge at home is a different supply, not this port running slowly. An alarm clock does not speak those protocols.

    • If the phone is warm and the percentage crawls, move it to a real charger. All night on 5W does not store a fast charge. It ends the night only partly ahead.

    The outlet is usually behind the furniture

    The USB jack sits where you will see it. The AC socket sits where the furniture fit. Look for a standard wall outlet before you decide the room has no power:

    • Behind the headboard, or between the bed and the nightstand. Pull the bed forward a few inches.
  • Under the desk, on the panel facing the wall, or in a pop-up grommet in the desktop.
  • Behind the TV stand or the dresser, low on the wall, sometimes beside the television cord.
  • A floor lamp or bathroom outlet is a phone backup, not a place to leave a laptop.

    • One wall socket is enough. Abroad, adapt the prongs: a travel 65W USB-C PD charger is normally 100–240V. The USB port on the TV is not a substitute. If the socket you can see is dead, test with your charger — some rooms switch every outlet from the panel by the door. When every wall socket is dead, ask for another room or a grounded extension and use a power bank until then. Five watts will not refill a laptop by breakfast.

    One wall outlet and a 65W charger

    Plug a 65W USB-C PD GaN charger into that outlet and a rated USB-C cable into the laptop. Use the second port for the phone, or charge the phone after the laptop has what you need for the morning.

    65W matches what many 13- and 14-inch laptops shipped with, so the battery climbs during ordinary work. A phone often draws 15–30W, then tapers. Ports share one budget, so the laptop can take about 45W and the phone the rest. That is a normal night, not two laptops at full speed, and not a 100W brick for a day of rendering.

    GaN is why that wattage fits in a bag. Gallium nitride wastes less energy as heat, so 65W fits a much smaller brick than the silicon adapter from the laptop box. The material side is in our GaN chargers explainer. In the room you get one socket and a charger that fits a side pocket.

    • A 15- or 16-inch laptop that shipped with a 96W or 100W adapter charges more slowly on 65W, or holds steady while you work. Close the lid when the percentage needs to rise.
  • A thin phone cable can cap the laptop well under 65W. Use a USB-C cable rated 100W or 5A between the charger and the computer.
  • If the laptop needs the full 65W, charge the phone second. One swap still beats leaving the laptop on the alarm clock.

    • Packing one brick for scarce outlets in general is our one 65W charger travel guide. A hotel is the specific case: a USB port in the furniture does not count as the outlet.

    What to pack, and what to skip

    1. 1. One 65W USB-C PD GaN charger with at least two ports, and a plug or a simple adapter for the country.
  • 2. One USB-C to USB-C cable rated for laptop power, not a thin giveaway lead.
  • 3. A phone cable if the phone is not USB-C, so you are not unplugging the laptop to read a message.
  • 4. A power bank for the gaps without AC — the lobby, the train, a socket you cannot reach. It does not replace the wall charger for a laptop top-up. Fly with it in carry-on; the watt-hour rules are in our flying with a power bank guide.

    • Keep the charger on top of the bag. Our travel backpack packing guide calls that the first-night pocket. Leave the extra phone brick at home — the 65W charger already charges the phone — and ignore a TV port that only proved it can feed earbuds at about 1–2W. Skip a warm cube tap on a lamp socket.

    The bottom line

    Nightstand, TV, and alarm-clock USB ports will not charge a laptop, and they often leave a phone only partly ahead by morning. Plan on about 5W from USB-A, and do not treat a USB-C jack in the same furniture as laptop Power Delivery. Find one real wall outlet — behind the bed, the desk, or the TV stand — and let a compact 65W USB-C PD GaN charger run both devices from that socket.
